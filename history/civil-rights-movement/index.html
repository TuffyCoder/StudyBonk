The Civil Rights Movement: Key Events, Figures & Laws

From Brown v. Board to the Voting Rights Act — the events, leaders and laws, plus a dates trick.

The movement begins: Brown and the bus

After Reconstruction, the South built Jim Crow: laws enforcing racial segregation in schools, transport, restaurants — nearly everything — under the lie of 'separate but equal.' Black Americans fought the system through courts, boycotts and brave daily acts. In May 1954, the Supreme Court's Brown v. Board of Education ruling struck down school segregation, declaring separate schools 'inherently unequal' and reversing nearly 60 years of legal doctrine.

In December 1955, Rosa Parks was arrested in Montgomery, Alabama, for refusing to give up her bus seat to a white passenger. The Black community responded with a 381-day bus boycott, organized in part by a 26-year-old pastor named Martin Luther King Jr. The boycott starved the bus system until the Supreme Court ordered desegregated buses — proving that organized nonviolent protest could win.

  • Jim Crow: state laws enforcing segregation, 'separate but equal'
  • Brown v. Board, May 1954: school segregation ruled unconstitutional
  • Rosa Parks, December 1955: arrest sparks the Montgomery Bus Boycott
  • 381 days of walking won desegregated buses
  • MLK emerges as the movement's leading voice

Nonviolence on the march: 1960 to 1963

The movement's playbook was nonviolent direct action — sit-ins, freedom rides, marches — designed to expose injustice by meeting violence with discipline. In 1960, college students launched sit-ins at segregated lunch counters (starting in Greensboro, North Carolina); in 1961, Freedom Riders rode integrated buses through the South and were beaten for it. In 1963, King's campaign in Birmingham, Alabama — where police turned dogs and fire hoses on children — shocked a nation watching on TV.

That August, about 250,000 people marched on Washington for jobs and freedom, where King delivered his 'I Have a Dream' speech at the Lincoln Memorial. The images were irresistible: the movement's discipline against segregation's brutality. President Kennedy had proposed a civil rights bill; after his assassination, President Lyndon Johnson pushed it through as the Civil Rights Act of 1964, banning segregation in public places and employment discrimination.

  • Sit-ins (1960) and Freedom Rides (1961): students lead
  • Birmingham 1963: dogs and fire hoses on TV changed opinions
  • March on Washington, August 1963: 'I Have a Dream'
  • Civil Rights Act of 1964: bans segregation in public places and job discrimination
  • Nonviolence as strategy: expose injustice, win the nation

Votes and legacy: Selma to 1968

Segregation's other pillar was voting denial: literacy tests, poll taxes and violence kept Black Southerners from the polls. In March 1965, marchers setting out from Selma, Alabama, were beaten by police on the Edmund Pettus Bridge — 'Bloody Sunday,' broadcast nationwide. A protected second march reached Montgomery, and that August the Voting Rights Act of 1965 banned the discriminatory tests and sent federal registrars South.

The movement won its central legal battles but at a cost: King was assassinated in Memphis on April 4, 1968, at age 39, and the Fair Housing Act passed days later as a memorial. The movement's methods — nonviolent mass protest, legal challenges, boycotts — became the template for nearly every justice movement since, and its unfinished business still shapes American life today.

  • Selma marches, March 1965: 'Bloody Sunday' shocks the nation
  • Voting Rights Act, August 1965: bans literacy tests and poll taxes
  • MLK assassinated April 4, 1968, in Memphis
  • Fair Housing Act, 1968: the era's final major law
  • Legacy: the template for modern rights movements
💡 How to remember this timeline

Drill five numbers: '54 school (Brown), '55 bus (Montgomery), '63 speech (March on Washington), '64 public places (Civil Rights Act), '65 votes (Voting Rights Act). School, bus, speech, places, votes — each year one step toward full citizenship.

Key concepts to memorize

Segregation / Jim CrowLaws enforcing racial separation in public life under the fiction of 'separate but equal.'
Brown v. Board of EducationThe 1954 Supreme Court ruling that school segregation is unconstitutional — separate is inherently unequal.
BoycottRefusing to use or buy from a business to force change; the Montgomery boycott lasted 381 days.
Nonviolent resistanceProtest without violence, meeting brutality with discipline — the movement's core strategy.
Sit-inProtesters occupying segregated seats and refusing to leave until served or arrested.
March on WashingtonThe August 1963 mass protest where MLK delivered 'I Have a Dream.'
Civil Rights Act of 1964Federal law banning segregation in public accommodations and employment discrimination.
Voting Rights Act of 1965Federal law banning literacy tests and protecting Black voting rights.

People also ask

Questions students also ask

What was the civil rights movement?
The 1950s–60s struggle to end legal segregation and secure equal rights for Black Americans — through courts, boycotts, marches and landmark federal laws.
Who were the key figures of the civil rights movement?
Martin Luther King Jr., Rosa Parks, Thurgood Marshall (Brown's lead lawyer, later the first Black Supreme Court justice), John Lewis, Fannie Lou Hamer — with Presidents Kennedy and Johnson at the legislative end.
What did Martin Luther King Jr. do?
Organized the Montgomery boycott, co-led the SCLC, led Birmingham and the March on Washington, won the 1964 Nobel Peace Prize — and was assassinated in 1968 at 39.
What ended legal segregation?
The combination of court rulings (Brown), mass protest (boycotts, sit-ins, marches) and federal law (the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965).
FAQ

Questions about civil rights movement

What started the civil rights movement?
Decades of organizing against Jim Crow burst into mass movement in the 1950s: the Supreme Court's Brown ruling (1954) gave the legal opening, and the Montgomery Bus Boycott (1955–56) gave the mass-protest template and its leader, MLK.
Why were the Selma marches important?
Bloody Sunday — police beating peaceful marchers on camera — horrified the country and directly built support for the Voting Rights Act, signed five months later. Selma turned voting rights from demand into law.
What's the difference between the 1964 and 1965 acts?
The 1964 act attacked segregation: public places and employment discrimination. The 1965 act attacked voting denial: literacy tests, poll taxes, federal protection of Black voters. One opened doors; the other opened ballot boxes.
Was the movement only in the South?
No. Segregation and discrimination were national — Northern cities faced housing and school battles too. The South's Jim Crow system was the movement's main target, but its victories reshaped the whole country.
