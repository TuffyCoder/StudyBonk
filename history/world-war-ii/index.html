World War II: Causes, Turning Points & Aftermath

Causes, turning points and aftermath of 1939–1945 — the war that remade the world.

How it happened again: the causes

World War II grew directly from World War I's wreckage. The Treaty of Versailles humiliated Germany; the Great Depression threw millions out of work worldwide; and in that desperation, fascist leaders promised national greatness — Hitler in Germany, Mussolini in Italy, militarists in Japan. Fascism promised order and glory at the cost of freedom, and it blamed scapegoats, especially Jewish people.

The democracies tried appeasement — giving in to demands to avoid another war. Britain and France let Hitler rearm and annex Austria, then at Munich in 1938 handed him Czechoslovakia's borderlands ('peace for our time' lasted under a year). When Germany and the USSR signed a surprise nonaggression pact, Hitler invaded Poland on September 1, 1939 — and Britain and France declared war two days later.

  • Versailles resentment + Great Depression = fascism's opening
  • Axis: Germany, Italy, Japan · Allies: Britain, USSR, US, China, France and more
  • Appeasement: giving in to Hitler's demands to avoid war
  • Munich 1938: Czechoslovakia sacrificed for 'peace'
  • Germany invades Poland, September 1, 1939: the war begins
💡 How to remember this timeline

Europe's story in five numbers: 1939 Poland falls (war begins) · 1941 Pearl Harbor (the US enters) · 1942–43 Stalingrad (the tide turns in the east) · 1944 D-Day (the western front opens) · 1945 Germany and Japan surrender. Chant it: Poland, Pearl, Stalingrad, D-Day, done.

Turning points: Pearl Harbor to V-J Day

December 7, 1941: Japan attacked Pearl Harbor, killing about 2,400 Americans and ending US neutrality overnight. In 1942 the tide turned twice. In the Pacific, the Battle of Midway (June 1942) destroyed Japan's carrier advantage and put it on defense. On the Eastern Front, the months-long Battle of Stalingrad (1942–43) ended with an entire German army surrendering — the war's bloodiest battle and the death of Germany's advance. Behind the lines, the Holocaust — Nazi Germany's systematic murder of six million Jewish people, plus millions of others — continued, the war's deepest crime.

Then D-Day — June 6, 1944 — landed Allied forces on the Normandy beaches, opening the western front. The liberation of Western Europe rolled east while the Soviets pushed west from Stalingrad. Germany surrendered in May 1945 (V-E Day). The Pacific war ended in August 1945 after the US dropped atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki; Japan formally surrendered September 2 (V-J Day).

  • Pearl Harbor: December 7, 1941 — the US enters the war
  • Midway, June 1942: the Pacific turning point
  • Stalingrad, 1942–43: the Eastern Front turning point
  • D-Day, June 6, 1944: the Allied landings in Normandy
  • V-E Day May 1945 · atomic bombs August 1945 · V-J Day September 1945

Aftermath: a remade world

The war killed roughly 70–85 million people, soldiers and civilians alike, and ended with the first — and so far only — wartime use of nuclear weapons. The Nuremberg trials held Nazi leaders accountable for newly defined 'crimes against humanity,' and the full horror of the Holocaust pushed human rights into international law, starting with the Universal Declaration of Human Rights.

Two superpowers stood above the rubble — the United States and the Soviet Union — and their rivalry became the Cold War. The United Nations (founded 1945) replaced the failed League of Nations, this time with the US in it. US Marshall Plan money rebuilt Western Europe while the USSR sealed off its half behind the Iron Curtain, and weakened European powers began losing their colonies. WWI remade maps; WWII remade the whole world order.

  • United Nations founded, 1945 — with the US in it this time
  • Nuremberg trials: leaders held accountable for war crimes
  • Marshall Plan: US aid rebuilt Western Europe
  • US and USSR emerge as rival superpowers → the Cold War
  • Decolonization: empires crumble over the next 30 years

Key concepts to memorize

FascismA dictatorial ideology glorifying the nation and military power, crushing democracy and blaming scapegoats.
AppeasementGiving in to an aggressor's demands to avoid war — symbolized by the 1938 Munich agreement.
Axis and AlliesAxis: Germany, Italy, Japan. Allies: Britain, the USSR, the US, China, France and others.
Blitzkrieg'Lightning war' — fast, coordinated tank-and-air assault; how Germany overran Poland and France.
HolocaustNazi Germany's systematic genocide of six million Jewish people and millions of others.
D-DayJune 6, 1944: the Allied invasion of Normandy that opened the western front in France.
V-E Day and V-J DayVictory in Europe (May 8, 1945) and Victory over Japan (September 2, 1945).
Marshall PlanUS economic aid that rebuilt Western Europe after the war — and built Cold War alliances.

🎯 Study tips for this topic

  • Chain the causes out loud: Versailles → depression → Hitler → appeasement → Poland. Tests love the chain.
  • Memorize the five-number spine: 1939, 1941, 1942–43, 1944, 1945.
  • Separate the two war stories — Europe vs. Pacific — then connect them at Pearl Harbor and V-J Day.
  • Flashcard the battles with their 'why it mattered' line, not just the date.
  • Practice one-sentence answers for 'how did WWII start?' and 'how did it end?' — those two questions carry most exams.

People also ask

Questions students also ask

What caused World War II?
Versailles humiliation, the Great Depression, fascist expansion, and appeasement that failed to stop it — triggered by Germany's invasion of Poland in 1939.
What was D-Day?
The June 6, 1944 Allied amphibious invasion of Normandy — history's largest seaborne assault, opening the front that liberated Western Europe.
How did World War II end?
Germany surrendered May 8, 1945, as Allied armies closed from east and west; Japan surrendered September 2, 1945, after the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki.
What was the Holocaust?
Nazi Germany's systematic genocide of six million Jewish people, alongside millions of Roma, disabled people, political prisoners and others — the war's central moral crime.
FAQ

Questions about world war ii

How was WWII different from WWI?
WWI was a static trench stalemate between great powers; WWII was a war of movement spanning the globe — blitzkrieg, air power, island-hopping — with clear turning points, city bombing, the Holocaust and nuclear weapons. WWII's death toll was several times higher.
Why did Japan attack Pearl Harbor?
The US had embargoed the oil and steel Japan needed for its empire, so Japan's leaders chose a preemptive strike to knock out the US Pacific Fleet. It backfired: the attack united Americans overnight and brought overwhelming US power into the war.
Why is Stalingrad considered the turning point?
It was among the largest and bloodiest battles in history and ended with a catastrophic German surrender — a whole army lost. After Stalingrad, Germany retreated on the Eastern Front until the war ended; it never regained the initiative.
What came out of WWII besides peace?
The United Nations, the Nuremberg precedent for war-crimes trials, the nuclear age, the Cold War rivalry, the decolonization wave and the modern human-rights framework — today's world order is WWII's aftermath.
