World War I: MAIN Causes, Trench Warfare & the Treaty of Versailles

The MAIN causes, life in the trenches and the treaty that accidentally set up round two.

The MAIN causes and the spark

Historians compress World War I's causes into the acronym MAIN: Militarism (building huge armies and glorifying military power), Alliances (Europe split into two armed camps, all promising to fight together), Imperialism (competing for colonies and global status), and Nationalism (intense pride that made every nation ready to fight for its side).

The spark: in June 1914, a Serbian nationalist assassinated Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to Austria-Hungary's throne, in Sarajevo. Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia; alliances yanked nation after nation in — Russia, Germany, France, Britain — and within about six weeks a Balkan shootout was a world war.

  • Militarism: an arms race and glorified military power
  • Alliances: everyone was chained to someone else
  • Imperialism: colonial rivalries added friction
  • Nationalism: every nation primed to defend its pride
  • The spark: Franz Ferdinand assassinated, June 1914
💡 How to remember this timeline

Four words: Spark, Surge, Stop, Sign. 1914 — the Spark (the assassination). 1917 — the Surge (the US enters). 1918 — the Stop (armistice on November 11, at the 11th hour). 1919 — the Sign (Treaty of Versailles). MAIN explains why it started; Spark-Surge-Stop-Sign tells you what happened.

Trench warfare and new technology

On the Western Front, neither side could push the other out of France and Belgium, so both dug in — literally. Soldiers lived in muddy, rat-infested trenches stretching for hundreds of miles, separated from the enemy by 'no man's land,' a shell-blasted strip that attacks had to cross. Machine guns, barbed wire and artillery made charges suicidal: battles lasted months and moved the front a few miles. Verdun and the Somme in 1916 together caused roughly two million casualties.

The war industrialized killing: poison gas, tanks, submarines (U-boats) and airplanes all debuted or matured. Germany's unrestricted U-boat warfare sank civilian ships — most famously the Lusitania in 1915 — and the leaked Zimmermann Telegram (Germany offering Mexico US territory) pushed a reluctant United States into the war in April 1917. Fresh American troops and supplies helped exhaust Germany into seeking an armistice.

  • Trenches + no man's land defined the Western Front
  • Machine guns, artillery and barbed wire froze the front lines
  • Verdun and the Somme (1916): months of fighting, tiny gains
  • New tech: poison gas, tanks, U-boats, airplanes
  • US enters April 1917 after U-boat attacks and the Zimmermann Telegram

The Treaty of Versailles and the aftermath

Germany, exhausted and blockaded, signed the armistice on November 11, 1918 — the 11th hour of the 11th day of the 11th month, now commemorated as Veterans Day. The 1919 Treaty of Versailles then dictated peace: Germany accepted blame for the war (the war guilt clause), lost territory and colonies, had its military capped, and owed crushing reparations. A new League of Nations was created to prevent future wars — but the US Senate refused to join, weakening it from birth.

The treaty's harshness backfired. Germans felt humiliated and betrayed, their economy collapsed under reparations and hyperinflation, and resentment fed extremist politics. Within twenty years, World War II grew from that wreckage — which is why historians call Versailles the peace that ended nothing.

  • Armistice: November 11, 1918, 11 a.m.
  • War guilt clause: Germany forced to accept blame
  • Reparations: massive payments that wrecked Germany's economy
  • League of Nations created — but the US never joined
  • Resentment over Versailles fed the rise of Hitler

Key concepts to memorize

MilitarismBuilding up armed forces and glorifying military power — one of the MAIN causes.
AlliancesMutual-defense promises that pulled nation after nation into a small conflict.
NationalismIntense pride in and loyalty to one's nation or ethnic group, ready to be defended by force.
Trench warfareArmies fighting from long dug-in trenches, facing each other across no man's land.
No man's landThe deadly strip between opposing trenches that attacks had to cross.
U-boatGerman submarine; unrestricted U-boat warfare helped pull the US into the war.
ArmisticeThe agreement ending the fighting, November 11, 1918.
Treaty of VersaillesThe 1919 peace treaty blaming and punishing Germany — sowing the grievances behind WWII.

People also ask

Questions students also ask

What caused World War I in simple terms?
MAIN: Militarism, Alliances, Imperialism, Nationalism — decades of buildup, ignited by the 1914 assassination of Franz Ferdinand.
When and how did WWI end?
Germany, exhausted and blockaded, signed an armistice on November 11, 1918; the Treaty of Versailles formally settled the peace in 1919.
Why did the US join World War I?
German submarines sank civilian and American ships, and the Zimmermann Telegram revealed Germany offering Mexico US land. War came in April 1917.
What was life like in the trenches?
Mud, rats, disease and shellfire in dug-in lines separated by no man's land — month-long battles moved the front only a few miles.
FAQ

Questions about world war i

Why was WWI called a 'world' war?
The fighting centered on Europe, but empires pulled in their colonies worldwide — troops from India, Africa, Australia and Canada fought — and battles reached the Middle East, Africa and the Pacific. Europe's empires made it global.
Why couldn't either side win in the trenches?
Defensive technology — machine guns, artillery, barbed wire — outran offensive tactics. Attacking across no man's land was nearly suicidal, so fronts barely moved for years until tanks, fresh American troops and exhaustion broke the stalemate.
What was the Zimmermann Telegram?
A secret 1917 German offer to Mexico: ally with Germany and win back territory lost to the US. Britain intercepted and shared it, outraging Americans and pushing the US into the war weeks later.
Was the Treaty of Versailles too harsh?
Most historians say it was harsh enough to humiliate and impoverish Germany yet too weak to keep it down long. The resentment it generated became fuel for Hitler's rise — some call it 'an armistice for twenty years.'
