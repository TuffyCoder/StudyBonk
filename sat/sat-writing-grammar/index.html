SAT Writing & Grammar: The Rules That Actually Get Tested

The small set of grammar rules the SAT actually tests — punctuation, agreement, modifiers, transitions and concision.

The rules the SAT actually tests

Good news: the SAT grammar categories are public. The Standard English Conventions questions cover sentence structure and boundaries (run-ons, fragments), punctuation (commas, semicolons, colons, dashes, apostrophes), agreement (subject-verb, pronoun-antecedent), verb tense and form, and modifiers. Expression of Ideas questions cover transitions, concision, and logical sentence placement.

That's it. You do not need to name grammatical terms like a linguistics professor — you need pattern recognition on about a dozen rules. Each rule shows up in nearly identical disguise every test, which makes grammar the most learnable, highest-ROI section of SAT prep. Two weeks of focused drilling routinely moves students 40+ points on Reading & Writing.

  • Boundaries: fixing run-ons, comma splices and fragments
  • Punctuation: commas, semicolons, colons, dashes, apostrophes
  • Agreement: subjects with verbs, pronouns with antecedents
  • Verbs: tense consistency and correct forms
  • Expression of Ideas: transitions, concision, sentence order

Punctuation: the highest-value rules

Learn commas by their jobs: they separate items in lists, set off extra information, and follow introductory phrases. They cannot join two complete sentences — that's a comma splice. To join two complete sentences you need a semicolon, a colon (when the second explains the first), a dash, or a comma plus a conjunction like 'and,' 'but' or 'so.' The colon rule trips the most students: what comes before a colon must be a complete sentence, and what follows elaborates — a list, definition or explanation.

Apostrophes are simpler than they look: use them for possession ('the student's book'; 'the students' books' for plural owners) and in contractions — never for simple plurals. When a choice offers 'its' versus 'it's,' expand the apostrophe into 'it is' and listen: if 'it is' makes no sense in the sentence, you want 'its.' That two-second test settles every its/it's, their/they're and whose/who's question ever written.

  • Comma splice: two full sentences joined by only a comma — always wrong
  • Semicolon: joins two complete, related sentences with no conjunction
  • Colon: complete sentence before; a list, definition or explanation after
  • Dashes work in pairs like parentheses to set off extra information
  • Its vs it's: expand to 'it is' — if it fits, use the apostrophe
💡 Worked example: fix the sentence

'Maria collected soil samples for weeks, the data surprised her advisor.' Two complete sentences, comma alone = comma splice. Fixes: '...for weeks; the data surprised...' or '...for weeks, and the data surprised...' or '...for weeks. The data surprised...' Recognizing that both halves are complete sentences is the whole battle.

Agreement, verbs and modifiers

For subject-verb agreement, find the true subject and ignore everything sandwiched between it and the verb. In 'The box of nails (were/was) heavy,' the subject is 'box,' not 'nails' — the prepositional phrase 'of nails' is camouflage. Same trick for pronouns: 'Each of the students left (their/his or her) notebook' — 'each' is singular, so match it. Scan for singular culprits like 'each,' 'every,' 'either' and 'neither.'

Dangling modifiers are wrong because the sentence never names who's doing the action. 'Walking to school, the rain soaked my jacket' claims the rain was walking. The fix is putting the real actor right after the comma: 'Walking to school, I was soaked by the rain.' If an underlined phrase describes an action, the very next words must name the person doing it.

  • Cross out prepositional phrases to expose the true subject
  • 'Each,' 'every,' 'either,' 'neither' are singular — match verbs accordingly
  • Tense should stay consistent unless the timeline genuinely shifts
  • Dangling modifier: the doer of the opening phrase must follow the comma
  • Pronouns must match their antecedent in number and clarity

Transitions and concision: the logic points

Transition questions test logic, not vocabulary. Read the two sentences, decide their relationship first — continuation ('similarly,' 'in addition'), contrast ('however,' 'nevertheless'), cause-effect ('therefore,' 'as a result'), or illustration ('for example') — then pick the matching choice. Deciding the relationship before looking at the answers is what keeps a fancy word like 'nonetheless' from seducing you when the logic is simple addition.

Concision questions ask which answer 'most effectively' completes the sentence, and the shortest grammatical answer is very often right — the test writers love redundancy like 'return back' and 'annual yearly tradition.' But shortest is a tiebreaker, not a law: the answer must first be grammatical and preserve the meaning. Kill redundancy, keep the meaning, and the point is yours.

  • Decide the sentence relationship before reading the choices
  • Contrast: however, nevertheless, in contrast
  • Cause-effect: therefore, thus, as a result
  • Redundancy kills: 'return back,' 'each individual person,' 'future plans'
  • Concision rule: shortest answer that is grammatical AND keeps the meaning

Key concepts to memorize

Comma spliceTwo complete sentences joined by only a comma — always incorrect on the SAT.
Independent clauseA complete sentence: subject plus verb, able to stand alone.
SemicolonJoins two closely related independent clauses without a conjunction.
ColonFollows a complete sentence and introduces a list, definition or explanation.
Subject-verb agreementThe verb matches the true subject once camouflage phrases are crossed out.
Dangling modifierA descriptive phrase whose doer never appears in the sentence — fix by naming the actor.
TransitionA word linking sentences by logic: contrast, cause-effect, continuation or example.
RedundancySaying the same thing twice ('end result') — the concision question's favorite target.

🎯 Study tips for this topic

  • Memorize the four fixes for comma splices (semicolon, colon, period, comma + conjunction) until spotting them is reflex.
  • Do 10 punctuation questions a day for a week — volume builds the pattern recognition these questions reward.
  • When checking agreement, physically cross out prepositional phrases in the booklet or on scratch paper.
  • For transition questions, write your own connector ('CONTRAST,' 'THEREFORE') in the margin before reading choices.

People also ask

Questions students also ask

What punctuation is tested on the SAT?
Commas, semicolons, colons, dashes and apostrophes — always in the service of joining or separating ideas correctly, never obscure rules.
How do you fix a run-on sentence?
Semicolon, colon, period, or comma plus a conjunction. All four fixes turn two fused sentences into legal English — pick based on the relationship between them.
What is tested in Expression of Ideas questions?
Logic and clarity: choosing transitions that match sentence relationships, cutting redundancy, and placing sentences where they fit the paragraph's flow.
Do SAT writing questions have 'no error' answers?
The digital SAT's structure makes every question a choice between concrete alternatives, so you're always picking the best version rather than blessing a sentence as error-free.
FAQ

Questions about sat writing & grammar

Which grammar rules does the SAT actually test?
A short, public list: sentence boundaries (run-ons and fragments), punctuation (commas, semicolons, colons, dashes, apostrophes), subject-verb and pronoun agreement, verb tense and form, modifiers, transitions and concision. Master these dozen patterns and you've covered every Standard English Conventions question.
How do I stop missing transition questions?
Stop choosing by sound. Cover the choices, read the two sentences, and label the relationship — contrast, cause-effect, addition or example. Then pick the choice matching your label. Deciding the logic first is what beats tempting-but-wrong words like 'nonetheless.'
Is the shortest answer always right on concision questions?
Usually, but only as a tiebreaker. The answer must first be grammatical and keep the full meaning. If the shortest choice drops information the sentence needs, it's wrong — redundancy is the target, not brevity for its own sake.
Can I learn SAT grammar in a week?
Yes, if it's a focused week: 30 minutes a day of one rule at a time (punctuation two days, agreement two days, transitions and concision two days, mixed review one). Grammar questions repeat their patterns so exactly that short, targeted drilling works unusually well.
