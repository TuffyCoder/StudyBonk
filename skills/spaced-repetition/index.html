Spaced Repetition: Study Less, Remember Longer

The forgetting curve, the intervals that beat it, and how StudyBonk schedules reviews for you automatically.

The forgetting curve (and how to fight it)

In the 1880s, psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus measured his own memory and found something brutal: forgetting is fast. Within 24 hours of learning something new, you lose the majority of it; within a week, almost all that remains is a stub. Plot retention over time and you get the forgetting curve — a cliff, then a slope, that every student falls off after every cram session.

Here's the counterintuitive rescue: reviewing right before you'd forget resets the curve, and each reset makes it flatter. Review today, and the memory now survives a few days. Review at three days, it survives a week or two. Each well-timed review roughly doubles the memory's survival time, so five short reviews spread over a month beat one marathon session on the same material — massively. That's spaced repetition: the same minutes, redistributed.

  • The forgetting curve: most new learning fades within 24-48 hours if untouched
  • A review just before forgetting flattens the curve dramatically
  • Each successful spaced review roughly doubles the memory's lifetime
  • Same total minutes, redistributed over days = dramatically more retention
  • Cramming works for tomorrow and fails for next month — the opposite trade

Intervals that actually work

You don't need an algorithm to start — a simple expanding ladder captures most of the benefit: learn it today, review tomorrow, then three days later, then a week, then two weeks, then monthly. The key principle is that each gap should be long enough that retrieval takes effort (that's the strengthening) but short enough that you mostly succeed. If you recall a card instantly and effortlessly, the interval was too short — stretch it.

Two practical rules keep it honest. First, failed reviews reset: a card you miss comes back tomorrow regardless of the ladder. Second, mixed review beats blocked: shuffle your cards so each retrieval is genuine recall, not 'the next one in the chapter sequence.' Write these intervals on paper for a week and you'll feel the system working — the cards you keep missing are exactly the ones you'd otherwise walk into an exam without.

  • Beginner ladder: learn → 1 day → 3 days → 1 week → 2 weeks → monthly
  • Interval rule: effortful but successful recall = perfect spacing
  • Miss a card? It resets to tomorrow — failure demands fast return
  • Shuffle decks: sequence cues turn recall into recognition
  • Instant-effortless recall means the interval was too short
💡 Try this tonight: the paper tracker

Take 10 flashcards (or 10 missed problems from today). Write today's date next to each. Review them tomorrow — check off the ones you got right (next review: +3 days) and circle the misses (next review: tomorrow). Track on paper for two weeks and watch the pattern: most cards stretch out, a stubborn few keep circling back — and those stubborn few are exactly where your points were leaking.

Using spacing beyond flashcards

Flashcards are the classic vehicle, but the principle applies to everything. Keep a problem log: every math or physics problem you miss goes in it, and you re-solve (not re-read) those problems after 3 days, then a week. For essays and reading, use the question-sandwich: write questions right after reading, answer them from memory two days later. Even your notes benefit: re-summarize each lecture from memory a day later, then check.

The final-exam version of all this is the beauty part: start spacing review 3-4 weeks out, and finals week becomes a review of mostly-known material instead of a re-learning panic. Students who space consistently report the strange experience of the exam containing things they feel like they've always known — which is just what a flattened forgetting curve feels like from the inside.

  • Problem log: re-SOLVE missed problems after 3 days and a week
  • Re-summarize lectures from memory a day later, then check against notes
  • Question sandwich on readings: write now, answer from memory in 2 days
  • Start final-exam spacing 3-4 weeks early for cumulative subjects
  • Flattened forgetting curve feels like 'I've always known this'

Key concepts to memorize

Forgetting curveEbbinghaus's finding that memory decays steeply — most within a day or two — without review.
Spaced repetitionReviewing material at expanding intervals, each review landing just before forgetting.
Expanding intervalGrowing gaps between reviews (1 day → 3 → 7 → 14 → 30) that flatten the curve.
Review resetA missed item returns to a short interval regardless of the schedule.
RetentionThe fraction of learned material still retrievable at a given time.
CrammingMassed study that fills short-term memory — effective for tomorrow, gone next month.
Daily queueThe auto-generated set of cards due today; the output of scheduling systems like StudyBonk's.
Distributed practiceThe general term for spreading study over time; one of learning science's strongest effects.

🎯 Study tips for this topic

  • Never learn new material the night before a test without one same-week review — the curve shows what that costs.
  • Cap daily review at what fits in 10-15 minutes; if the queue overflows, trim the deck, not the habit.
  • Do reviews first, new cards second — aging memories are the ones about to fall off the cliff.
  • Convert every missed exam question into a card or log entry the same day, while the gap still stings.
  • Start finals spacing 3-4 weeks out: one subject per day on rotation, all review, no re-learning.

People also ask

Questions students also ask

Why do I forget everything after the test?
Because cramming loads short-term memory, which empties on a cliff-like schedule once the pressure disappears. Reviews spaced across the following days convert it to long-term memory instead.
What is the difference between spaced repetition and cramming?
Distribution over time. Cramming masses all exposure in one block (great for tomorrow, gone next week); spacing spreads the same minutes across days so each review catches the memory just before it fades.
How does StudyBonk's spaced repetition work?
Every card you answer updates its schedule — known cards wait longer, missed cards return sooner — and a daily queue assembles exactly what's due. You just show up and the forgetting curve gets fought on schedule.
Is Anki the only way to do spaced repetition?
No — it's one tool. StudyBonk runs spaced repetition with no sign-up, and even a paper calendar with expanding review dates delivers most of the same benefit. The method is the timing, not the software.
FAQ

Questions about spaced repetition

How long until spaced repetition starts working?
Immediately for scheduling, and noticeably within a week or two for feels: reviews start landing when memories are wobbly-but-retrievable. The wow moment is usually a monthly review of material you haven't touched in weeks that still comes back in seconds.
How many flashcards should I review a day?
Whatever fits in 10-15 minutes once the queue is churning — for most students that's 30-60 mature cards or 10-20 new ones plus reviews. If the queue regularly overflows, shrink the deck: a daily habit on fewer cards beats an abandoned habit on many.
Does spaced repetition work for math and science?
Yes — use missed problems instead of cards: log every problem you get wrong, then re-solve it (not re-read) after 3 days and again after a week. Spacing plus retrieval works on procedures and concepts, not just facts.
Can I do spaced repetition without an app?
Absolutely — a paper tracker with dates next to each item, reviewed on the 1-3-7-14-30 ladder, captures most of the benefit. Apps like StudyBonk just remove the scheduling chore, which is where most manual systems die.
