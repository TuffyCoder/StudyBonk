Magnetism Explained: Magnetic Fields, Electromagnets & Motors

Poles, fields, electromagnets and motors — magnetism from fridge magnets to fans.

Magnetic poles and fields

Every magnet has two poles: north and south. Like poles repel; opposite poles attract — you've felt magnets shove each other away. Break a magnet in half and you don't get a lone north: you get two smaller complete magnets. Poles always come in pairs.

A magnetic field is the invisible region of influence around a magnet, drawn as field lines leaving the north pole and curving around to the south. Iron filings sprinkled on paper over a bar magnet trace these lines — one of the best five-minute physics demos there is. The field is strongest where the lines crowd together: at the poles.

  • North–south attract; north–north and south–south repel
  • Breaking a magnet gives two smaller magnets, never a lone pole
  • Field lines run out of north and into south, outside the magnet
  • Where lines crowd close, the field is strongest (the poles)
  • Iron, nickel and cobalt (ferromagnetic) are the magnetic materials

Electromagnets: magnetism from electricity

In 1820, Hans Oersted noticed a compass needle twitch beside a current-carrying wire — electricity and magnetism are two faces of one thing. Wrap a wire into a coil and send current through it, and you get a magnetic field like a bar magnet's. Add an iron core and the field concentrates: that's an electromagnet.

Electromagnets beat permanent magnets because you can switch them on and off, reverse them, and tune their strength. Strength grows with more turns of wire, more current and a better core. Junkyard cranes, speakers, doorbells, MRI machines and maglev trains all run on that controllability.

  • Electric current always creates a magnetic field around it
  • Electromagnet = coil of wire + current, usually with an iron core
  • More turns, more current, better core = stronger
  • On/off and reversible — the superpower permanent magnets lack
💡 Worked example: doubling the coils

For a science project, an electromagnet with 50 coils picks up 12 paper clips. You double the turns to 100, and it picks up about 24 — strength scales roughly with the number of turns, so coil count is the cheapest upgrade. Then bump the current with a second battery and watch the count climb again (and the wire warm up).

How motors work

A motor is the loop-the-loop child of electricity and magnetism. Put a current-carrying wire inside a magnetic field, and the field pushes the wire sideways — that's the motor effect. Bend the wire into a loop and the push twists one side up and the other down, spinning the loop.

The trick that keeps it spinning: just as the loop lines up with the field and would stop, the commutator — a split-ring contact — flips the current direction, which flips the push, and the loop keeps rotating. Add more loops and you get smoother, stronger rotation. Every fan, drill, EV wheel and fridge compressor is this idea industrialized.

  • Current + magnetic field = sideways force on the wire
  • Loop of current in a field = torque = rotation
  • Commutator flips the current every half-turn to keep the spin going
  • More loops = smoother, stronger rotation
  • Same physics in fans, drills, hard drives and electric cars

Key concepts to memorize

Magnetic poleOne of the two ends of a magnet, north or south; poles always come in pairs.
Magnetic fieldThe region of magnetic influence around a magnet, mapped by field lines.
FerromagneticMaterials like iron, nickel and cobalt that are strongly attracted to magnets.
Magnetic domainTiny regions in a material where atomic magnetism aligns; aligned domains = magnetized.
ElectromagnetA coil of wire whose current produces a magnetic field, usually boosted by an iron core.
CommutatorThe split-ring switch that reverses current direction each half-turn in a motor.
Motor effectThe sideways force on a current-carrying wire inside a magnetic field.
CompassA small magnet that aligns with Earth's magnetic field, pointing roughly north.

People also ask

Questions students also ask

What makes something magnetic?
Its atoms' tiny magnetic domains line up instead of canceling out. In iron, nickel and cobalt this happens easily — hence fridge magnets stick to steel, not plastic or aluminum.
How do you make an electromagnet at home?
Wrap insulated wire many times around a large iron nail and connect the ends to a battery. More wraps and more current = stronger pickup. Disconnect to switch it off.
How does an electric motor work in simple terms?
A current-carrying loop sits in a magnetic field, gets pushed, spins; a commutator flips the current each half-turn so the push never reverses the spin. Repeat thousands of times a minute and you have a drill.
What's the difference between a permanent magnet and an electromagnet?
Permanent magnets (fridge magnets) are always on. Electromagnets are on only while current flows — controllable, reversible, scalable, which is why industry runs on them.
FAQ

Questions about magnetism

Are electricity and magnetism related?
Deeply — they're two sides of one force called electromagnetism. Moving charge creates a magnetic field, and changing magnetic fields can push charge. Electromagnets, motors and generators all live on this connection.
Why does a compass point north?
Earth behaves like a giant magnet thanks to its churning molten iron outer core. The compass needle — itself a tiny magnet — lines up with Earth's field. It points to magnetic north, slightly off from true geographic north.
Can you turn an electromagnet off?
Yes — cut the current and the field collapses. That on/off control (plus reversal and strength tuning) is exactly why cranes can drop scrap metal and doorbells can ding.
How does a motor keep spinning instead of stopping?
The loop would stop once it aligned with the field — but the commutator flips the current direction at that moment, flipping the force and keeping the rotation going. It's a perfectly timed electrical U-turn.
