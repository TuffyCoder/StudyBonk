Mitosis vs Meiosis: The Differences, Finally Clear

Two identical copies vs four unique gametes — the mitosis/meiosis comparison made permanent.

Why cells divide two different ways

Your body runs two separate division programs for two separate jobs. Mitosis makes new cells for growth and repair — replacing skin, healing cuts, growing taller. Meiosis makes sex cells (sperm and eggs), called gametes, and exists purely for reproduction.

The results are opposites. Mitosis: one cell splits into two identical cells with the same chromosome number as the parent. Meiosis: one cell splits twice into four cells, each with half the chromosomes — because sperm and egg combine at fertilization to restore the full count. Keep 'two for the body, four for the babies' in mind and the details fall into place.

  • Mitosis: growth and repair — makes new body cells
  • Meiosis: making gametes (sperm and egg) for reproduction
  • Mitosis → 2 identical cells; meiosis → 4 unique cells
  • Meiosis halves the chromosome number; fertilization restores it

Mitosis: copy, line up, split

Mitosis has four named phases, PMAT: prophase (chromosomes condense and become visible), metaphase (they line up in the middle), anaphase (sister chromatids are pulled to opposite ends) and telophase (two new nuclei form). Cytokinesis then pinches the cell into two.

The outcome is clones: every resulting body cell has the exact same DNA as the original. That's what you want for repair — a skin cell should make another skin cell, not a surprise. Memorize PMAT as a story — get ready, line up, pull apart, rebuild — and sequence questions become free points.

  • PMAT: Prophase, Metaphase, Anaphase, Telophase
  • Metaphase = middle · anaphase = apart (matching first letters)
  • Cytokinesis: the actual splitting of the cell after PMAT
  • Result: 2 genetically identical diploid cells

Meiosis: two splits, four gametes

Meiosis runs PMAT twice — meiosis I and meiosis II — turning one cell into four. The critical event happens early, in prophase I: crossing over, where matching chromosomes swap chunks of DNA. Combined with the random way chromosome pairs separate, that shuffling is why every gamete — and every sibling — is genetically unique.

Human body cells are diploid (46 chromosomes); meiosis produces haploid gametes with 23. When sperm meets egg, 23 + 23 = 46 again. Note that meiosis is not just mitosis done twice: the first division separates chromosome pairs, the second separates sister chromatids — different events with different outcomes.

  • Meiosis = PMAT twice (meiosis I and meiosis II)
  • Crossing over in prophase I swaps DNA between chromosome pairs
  • Result: 4 haploid cells (23 chromosomes each in humans), all unique
  • Fertilization restores the diploid number: 23 + 23 = 46
💡 Worked example: how many chromosomes?

A human muscle cell has 46 chromosomes. After mitosis, each new cell has 46 — identical copies. After meiosis, each gamete has 23. If a horse's body cells have 64 chromosomes, its gametes have 32: halving is the rule, whatever the starting number.

The side-by-side comparison (test-ready)

The compare-and-contrast table is where the points live. Mitosis: one division, 2 cells, identical, diploid, body cells, growth and repair. Meiosis: two divisions, 4 cells, unique, haploid, gametes only, reproduction. If a question mentions genetic variation, the answer is meiosis — full stop.

Watch for the traps. Crossing over and haploid results belong only to meiosis. 'Identical daughter cells' belongs only to mitosis. And meiosis happens in special locations (ovaries and testes in animals), while mitosis happens almost anywhere you have cells. Anchor each keyword to one process and you can't be tricked.

  • Mitosis: 1 division · 2 cells · identical · diploid · growth/repair
  • Meiosis: 2 divisions · 4 cells · unique · haploid · gametes
  • Keywords 'variation,' 'crossing over,' 'gamete' → meiosis
  • Keywords 'identical,' 'repair,' 'clone' → mitosis

Key concepts to memorize

MitosisCell division producing two genetically identical diploid cells for growth and repair.

ChromosomeA coiled package of DNA; humans have 46 in each body cell.
DiploidFull chromosome number (46 in humans) — two complete sets, one from each parent.
HaploidHalf the chromosome number (23 in humans) — one set, found in gametes.
GameteA sex cell (sperm or egg), haploid, that combines at fertilization.
Crossing overThe swap of DNA segments between chromosome pairs in prophase I of meiosis.
CytokinesisThe final splitting of the cell cytoplasm into two separate cells.

People also ask

Questions students also ask

How many cells result from meiosis?
Four haploid cells, each genetically unique — versus two identical cells from mitosis.
What happens during crossing over?
Paired chromosomes swap segments of DNA during prophase I, mixing genes so each gamete carries a brand-new combination.
Where does mitosis occur in the body?
Almost everywhere — skin, blood, bones, healing wounds. Any tissue that grows or repairs itself is running mitosis.
Can a human cell have 23 chromosomes?
Yes — gametes (sperm and egg) do. That haploid number is the whole point of meiosis: halve now so fertilization can restore 46.
FAQ

Questions about mitosis vs meiosis

What is the main difference between mitosis and meiosis?
Mitosis makes two identical body cells with the full chromosome number for growth and repair. Meiosis makes four unique gametes with half the number for reproduction. Identical vs unique is the fastest test: identical = mitosis, unique = meiosis.
Is meiosis just mitosis twice?
No — the two divisions are different events. Meiosis I separates chromosome pairs (and includes crossing over); meiosis II separates sister chromatids, like mitosis does. That's why the result is four halved, shuffled cells rather than four clones.
Why does meiosis matter for variation?
Crossing over mixes DNA between chromosomes, and each pair separates randomly. Every gamete gets a unique combination — which is why siblings (except identical twins) are genetically different from each other and from their parents.
How can I remember the phases of mitosis?
PMAT as a story: prophase gets ready (chromosomes appear), metaphase meets in the middle, anaphase pulls apart, telophase rebuilds two nuclei. Two mnemonics inside: metaphase-middle and anaphase-apart.
